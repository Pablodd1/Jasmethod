# JMM nutrition evidence and rule specification

Research checked: 7 October 2026. Scope: running (including sprint), cycling, swimming, triathlon, HYROX, and supporting resistance training. This is research and a proposed decision specification, not an individualized prescription. Targeted source search, not a registered exhaustive systematic review. Numerical protocols describe studied/consensus conditions; they are not guarantees of benefit or medical clearance.

## Findings that change the product

1. Fueling should depend on duration, intensity, prior intake, recovery interval, GI tolerance and access to food. An unconditional 60–80 g carbohydrate/hour recommendation is indefensible.
2. Evidence now extends beyond 90 g/h: a June 2026 crossover study found better post-3-hour critical power at 120 versus 60 g/h. This does not establish a universal optimum, and it did not compare 90 with 120 g/h. A 2025 elite-male-running study measured metabolism/economy, not faster marathon performance.
3. Sweat sodium loss is an exposure estimate, not a replacement prescription. Fluids, duration, diet, renal handling, environment and tolerability matter. Sodium cannot make overdrinking safe.
4. Creatine is a strong resistance-training/repeated-high-intensity aid, not a proven universal swim-sprint or distance-running aid. Beta-alanine laboratory capacity benefits do not reliably predict elite swimming race benefits.
5. No fixed female-versus-male carb, fluid, sodium or supplement multiplier. Underrepresented populations and uncertain menstrual-phase effects require transparent uncertainty rather than invented precision.
6. HYROX advice is substantially extrapolated from endurance, intermittent exercise and strength evidence. Do not label a general ergogenic aid “HYROX-proven.”

## Evidence labels

- **A: established in a defined use case**: convergent human trials and major professional consensus. Does not mean every sport/person benefits.
- **B: conditional/emerging**: small or heterogeneous trials, restricted endpoints, or meaningful implementation limits.
- **C: insufficient for routine performance use**: inconsistent evidence, unsupported extrapolation, surrogate-only findings or no relevant trials located.
- **M: medical indication only**: correction of assessed deficiency or clinical need; clinician-managed.
- **X: exclude**: prohibited, unsafe, unverified composition, or unsupported high-risk practice.

Each rule needs two independent fields: evidence strength and applicability. Example: creatine can be A for resistance-training adaptation and C for improving a single elite swim sprint.

## Fueling evidence matrix

| Use case | Evidence-based range / timing | Endpoint and applicability | Implementation cautions / evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily carbohydrate | Conventional planning ranges: light/skill days 3–5 g/kg/day; moderate ~1 h/day 5–7; endurance ~1–3 h/day 6–10; very high load ~4–5 h/day 8–12. These are planning ranges, not mandatory ceilings or calorie prescriptions. | A for sufficient carbohydrate supporting glycogen-demanding training; actual need follows work and energy balance. | Requires actual body mass and actual load. Do not infer body mass from sex, sport or a sample athlete. Include exercise carbs in the daily total. [Thomas et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852). |
| Before competition / demanding prolonged exercise | 1–4 g/kg carbohydrate 1–4 h before, adapted to time available and tolerance. Carbohydrate loading for events >90 min: 10–12 g/kg/day for 36–48 h with reduced training, if appropriate. | A: glycogen availability; loading is relevant to prolonged competition, not every sprint or gym visit. | Smaller/less fibrous, lower-fat choices close to start can be easier to tolerate. Do not force maximum intake. No requirement for a glycogen-depletion phase. [Burke et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2011.585473). |
| During <45 min | No routine exogenous carbohydrate requirement for performance when adequately fueled. | A/conditional: short effort fuel comes predominantly from existing stores; full session and repeated rounds still matter. | “No during-session requirement” must not become “skip meals.” |
| During ~45–75 min hard exercise | Small amounts or carbohydrate mouth exposure/rinse may help; no universal g/h target. | A/B: central/perceptual effects; benefit context-dependent, particularly feeding state. | Optional rather than essential. Do not recommend mouth rinsing while swimming or in unsafe situations. |
| During ~1–2.5 h sustained/intermittent demanding exercise | Common supported planning band 30–60 g/h. | A for endurance performance; includes long run/bike and many prolonged training sessions. | Start from tolerated intake and food access; do not treat 30 g/h as mandatory for easy, well-fed sessions. |
| During >2.5–3 h demanding exercise | Up to ~90 g/h using multiple-transportable carbohydrate is well-established; build and rehearse tolerance. | A for appropriate prolonged events. | Absolute g/h rather than automatic g/kg/h. These duration bins overlap intentionally; avoid a discontinuity at minute 75 or 150. [Burke carbohydrate guidance](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2011.585473), [Thomas position statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26891166/). |
| Advanced long-event fueling >90 to 120 g/h | Optional specialist-supervised, rehearsed strategy rather than default. Different glucose/fructose formulations have been studied. | B: laboratory durability/metabolism and selected field recovery findings; stronger cycling feasibility than broad race-outcome evidence. | 2026 UCI race-nutrition review explicitly rejects universal application of ≥120 g/h and does not support simply scaling during-exercise intake by body mass. No routine >120 g/h recommendation. [UCI 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2025-0239). |
| Rapid glycogen recovery | When next demanding session is within ~8 h: 1.0–1.2 g/kg/h carbohydrate for first ~4 h is an established rapid-restoration strategy. | A: glycogen restoration; not a requirement after every workout. | With ample recovery time, adequate total food and carbohydrate across normal meals matters more than minute-level timing. [ISSN nutrient timing 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0189-4). |
| Protein for endurance + strength | General exercising-adult range 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day; distribute quality protein across meals. Studied meal target ~0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g, every 3–4 h. | A for supporting muscle protein synthesis/training adaptation when total needs are met. | Not a narrow 30-minute “anabolic window.” Protein powder is a convenience option; food works. Higher needs can be context-specific, not an automatic sex or age formula. [ISSN protein 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8). |

**Transport mechanism and gut practice.** Glucose and glucose polymers principally use SGLT1; fructose uses GLUT5. Combining transport pathways can raise exogenous carbohydrate oxidation beyond single-source feeding. “60 g/h glucose ceiling” and “90 g/h total ceiling” are useful historical approximations, not universal hard biological limits. More absorption/oxidation does not automatically equal more race performance. A glucose:fructose ratio of 2:1 is established; newer high-intake studies use ~1:0.8 or 1:1. Do not describe any one ratio as optimal for everyone. [Currell & Jeukendrup 2008](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31815adf19).

### Selected primary carbohydrate studies

| Study | Population / design / exposure | Outcome and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| [Currell & Jeukendrup 2008](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/superior-endurance-performance-with-ingestion-of-multiple-transpo/) | Cycling trial comparing glucose+fructose, glucose alone, and water; carbohydrate arms both 1.8 g/min (108 g/h). | Better subsequent endurance performance with mixed transportable carbohydrates. Foundational mechanism/performance evidence, not proof that 108 g/h is each athlete’s optimum. |
| [Costa et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28177715/), DOI 10.1139/apnm-2016-0453 | 25 endurance runners randomized to 2 weeks repeated carbohydrate food/gel gut challenge versus placebo. Challenge: 30 g every 20 min (90 g/h), 2 h running then 1 h distance test. | Repeated practice can improve tolerance and performance-related outcomes. Small study; do not promise that everyone tolerates 90 g/h within exactly 14 days. |
| [Miall et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.12912) | 18 runners; 2-week carbohydrate versus placebo gut-training intervention, 90 g/h test exposure. | Reduced GI symptoms/malabsorption supports training the exact race strategy. No justification for forcing symptoms or escalating all athletes to elite intake. |
| [Podlogar et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-022-05019-w) | 120 versus 90 g/h combined fructose-maltodextrin at different ratios. | Higher exogenous but unchanged endogenous carbohydrate oxidation. Metabolic evidence does not prove glycogen sparing or race-time superiority. |
| [Ravikanti et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41191077/), DOI 10.1152/japplphysiol.00665.2025 | 8 elite male marathoners; mean PB 2:22:54. 120-min running; 60 g/h maltodextrin, 90 g/h 2:1, 120 g/h 1:1. | Hour-2 exogenous oxidation 0.89/1.31/1.68 g/min, respectively; lower oxygen cost at 120 versus 60. Peak nausea/fullness/cramps highest at 120. No marathon race-time test; dose and composition changed together. Industry-funded; small male-only sample. |
| [Norte et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/sms.70326), online 19 June 2026 | 16 trained cyclists/triathletes (15 men, 1 woman; >5 h/week; mean VO₂max 51.8 mL/kg/min); 0, 60, 120 g/h with 1:0.8 maltodextrin:fructose during 3 h moderate cycling. | Critical power was estimated from end-test power in a 3-min all-out test: post-exercise 236/257/266 W versus 277 W fresh. W′ declined without between-fueled-condition benefit. Supports durability under this protocol; not a 90-versus-120 comparison or complete triathlon race test. |
| [Urdampilleta et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu12072094) | Trail marathon; randomized 60/90/120 g/h groups, 26 runners allocated. | Higher-dose findings concern internal load and 24-h neuromuscular/high-intensity recovery, not a universal optimal marathon race intake. Small field study and population-specific. |

## Hydration, sodium and heat

| Topic | Practical evidence rule | Strength / source / limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Begin normally hydrated | Ordinary meals and fluid; assess context using thirst, recent morning mass trend and urine observations together. No “clear urine all day” goal. | A/consensus: [NATA 2017](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-52.9.02). Urine alone is imperfect and influenced by foods, supplements and timing. |
| During exercise | Drink to thirst is a safe fallback for many situations. Planned drinking can be useful for prolonged/high-performance heat exposure or constrained access, based on measured behavior and losses. | Individualize; neither force a fixed L/h for everyone nor insist all elite athletes ignore planning. [EAH consensus 2015](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-095004); [IOC heat consensus 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2022-105942). |
| Sweat-rate estimate | Approximate sweat loss L = pre-exercise dry mass kg − post-exercise dry mass kg + drinks L − urine L. Divide by hours. | Not a clinical measurement or automatic drink target. Standardize scale/clothes, dry skin/hair, record all intake/output, repeat in relevant conditions. Long events add respiratory/metabolic mass error; swimming adds retained water error. [NATA](https://nata.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/attr/52/9/article-p877.xml). |
| Sweat sodium | If measured credibly, loss estimate mg/h = sweat L/h × whole-body sweat sodium mg/L. Conversion: mmol/L × 22.99 = mg/L sodium. | Report as estimated loss only. Patch/site collection and extrapolation can introduce error. Salt crust, sex, cramps and sweat volume do not independently provide a sodium concentration. |
| No 100% default replacement | Some mass loss during endurance activity is normal. Avoid a plan that causes fluid-driven weight gain; do not force replacement of every estimated mL or mg. | A for avoiding overdrinking; sodium cannot prevent dilutional hyponatremia with excess fluid. [EAH consensus](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-095004). |
| Sodium decision | Consider duration, fluid intake relative to losses, food/beverage sodium, palatability, environmental exposure and medical restrictions. Unknown sweat sodium → unknown loss, not a guessed 1,000 mg/L. | Current UCI review emphasizes context and no demonstrated blanket performance benefit. Sodium is not an automatic cramp cure. [UCI 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2025-0239). |
| Sodium direct trial | 9 well-trained cyclists (5 men/4 women), ~3-h road TT in ~14°C; salt supplementation versus placebo, ad-libitum drinking. | No performance difference (171 vs 172 min, p=.46). Do not generalize this to all hot ultra-events; it rebuts universal need. [Cosgrove & Black 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-10-30). |
| Recovery fluid | If rapid restoration is necessary and a deficit was actually measured, ~1.25–1.5 L/kg mass deficit over recovery with sodium-containing food/fluid can account for continued losses. | A/conditional. This is per kg **lost**, not per kg body mass. Ordinary meals/drinking usually suffice with longer recovery. Do not apply after weight gain or when hyponatremia is suspected. [NATA 2017](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-52.9.02). |
| Heat | Acclimation/acclimatization, pacing, shade/cooling, clothing and event medical procedures matter as well as fluids. Re-test sweat patterns as weather/acclimation changes. | [IOC 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2022-105942). Hydration does not guarantee prevention of heat stroke. Confusion, collapse or altered behavior during heat/exertion is an emergency; stop and obtain urgent help. |

Sodium recommendations are disputed in detail. A 2025 physiology-focused review links sodium decisions more closely to fluid balance and duration than to mandatory sweat-loss matching; it is a scholarly review, not a universal professional consensus. Keep this uncertainty visible rather than converting its model into a clinical calculator. [McCubbin 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s44410-025-00011-9).

## Supplements: dose, timing, duration, use case and safety

All protocols below require product identity, health/medication review, athlete preference and prior tolerance. None should automatically be added to a meal plan. Trial one change at a time in relevant training, not for the first time on race day. Performance percentage improvements from different studies must never be summed.

| Intervention | Studied / consensus protocol | Best-supported endpoint and applicability | Safety / exclusions / practical grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Caffeine | Low effective doses often ~2–3 mg/kg; much research uses 3–6 mg/kg ~60 min pre-exercise. Forms differ; gum and during/late-event dosing need form-specific timing. No loading cycle needed. | A for endurance and several intense/strength tasks; small variable individual effects. AIS favors lowest effective dose rather than “more is better.” | Insomnia, anxiety, tremor, tachycardia, GI effects; count coffee, gels, drinks and pre-workouts together. Avoid concentrated loose caffeine powders. Medication interactions, arrhythmia, uncontrolled BP, pregnancy/lactation or minors → qualified review, not automatic sport dosing. [AIS caffeine](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/caffeine/how-and-when-do-i-use-it); [IOC 2018](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027). |
| Creatine monohydrate | 3–5 g/day for ~4 weeks without loading; optional ~0.3 g/kg/day for ~5 days in divided doses, then 3–5 g/day. Timing is less important than consistent ingestion. | A for resistance-training strength/lean-mass adaptation and repeated high-intensity work. Not an acute pre-workout stimulant. | Water-associated mass increase and GI symptoms possible; weigh running/climbing trade-off. No credible need for exotic forms. Healthy-adult renal safety evidence should not be extrapolated to existing kidney disease or relevant medications. [AIS creatine](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/creatine); [ISSN 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0173-z). |
| Beta-alanine | Common 3.2–6.4 g/day; AIS practical protocol 1.6 g four times daily with meals/snack (6.4 g/day), ≥4 weeks. Sustained-release/divided doses reduce tingling. | A/B for selected high-intensity capacity/performance tasks ~30 s–10 min, strongest rationale for sustained/repeated glycolytic work. Not established as a direct long-course endurance booster. | Paresthesia; long-term data less comprehensive than creatine; no acute performance benefit from a pre-workout sprinkle. Do not promise response based on lower baseline carnosine in women. [AIS beta-alanine](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/b-alanine). |
| Dietary nitrate / beetroot | AIS commonly ~6–8 mmol nitrate (~372–496 mg; AIS rounds ~350–500 mg), ~2–3 h pre-exercise. Acute or repeated-day strategies. Expert Delphi 2022 discusses broader 8–16 mmol acute and 4–16 mmol/day chronic, final dose 2–4 h pre; these are different recommendations, not a reason to default to the highest dose. | B, conditional ergogenic aid: oxygen cost and some time-trial outcomes, less consistent in highly trained/elite endurance athletes. | Standardized nitrate content matters, not grams of beetroot powder or mL alone. GI discomfort, harmless beeturia; chronic safety uncertainty. Do not use nitrate/nitrite chemicals or fertilizer. Blood-pressure-related medication/clinical issues need clinician/pharmacist review. [AIS nitrate dose](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/beetroot-juicenitrate/what-does-it-look-like); [Delphi 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01701-3). |
| Sodium bicarbonate | Established acute research often 0.2–0.3 g/kg; AIS lists 0.2–0.4 g/kg with a carbohydrate-rich meal ~120–150 min beforehand, often spread over 30–60 min. Individual timing/tolerance testing matters. | A/B for certain sustained/repeated high-intensity efforts; select track running/cycling and swimming contexts. Not a routine distance-running/HYROX supplement. | Frequent nausea/diarrhea/bloating; large sodium load, alkalosis/electrolyte risk; renal/cardiac disease, hypertension/sodium restrictions, medications or recurrent GI problems require medical clearance. Do not offer kitchen-spoon dosing or self-directed serial loading. [AIS protocol](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/bicarbonate/how-and-when-do-i-use-it); [AIS tolerability](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/performance-supplements2/bicarbonate/are-there-any-concerns-or-considerations). |
| Iron | **M only.** AIS clinician framework commonly ~100 mg elemental iron daily or alternate mornings for ~8–12 weeks with repeat blood testing; this describes treatment, not an app recommendation. | Restore confirmed low iron status; benefit depends on deficiency and physiology. Not an ergogenic aid for iron-replete athletes. | Require clinician interpretation of ferritin, hemoglobin, transferrin saturation and inflammation/context. Investigate cause, not just replace. GI effects, overdose/toxicity, iron overload; no automatic iron from sex, vegetarian diet or fatigue. No unsupervised IV route. [AIS iron](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_a/medical-supplements2/iron/how-and-when-do-i-use-it). |
| Protein powder | Amount only to close a demonstrated food/protein gap; food-equivalent doses from protein row. | A as convenient nutrition, not inherently superior to food. | Allergens, tolerability, quality and batch verification; no BCAA requirement on top of adequate quality protein. |

### Primary supplement studies and limits

- **Nitrate:** [Lansley et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21471821/), DOI 10.1249/MSS.0b013e31821597b4: nine club-level male cyclists, ~6.2 mmol beetroot nitrate ~2.5 h before 4-/16.1-km time trials. This is relevant recreational/club evidence, not a sex-neutral elite guarantee.
- **Creatine swimming caution:** [Burke et al. 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8876342/), DOI 10.1123/ijsn.6.3.222: 32 elite swimmers (18 male/14 female), 20 g/day ×5 days; no significant single-sprint benefit. [Mujika et al. 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8933496/): 20 highly trained swimmers, similarly no 25-/50-/100-m improvement. Strong supporting-strength evidence does not erase direct null swim evidence.
- **Beta-alanine swimming, positive small trial:** [de Salles Painelli et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1139/apnm-2012-0286): 16 swimmers, 3.2 g/day week 1 then 6.4 g/day four weeks; 100-/200-m trial improvements ~2%. Small research setting, not a promised real-world effect.
- **Beta-alanine swimming, field limitation:** [Chung et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23201763/), DOI 10.3390/nu4101441: 41 elite/subelite swimmers (23 male/18 female), 4.8 g/day four weeks then 3.2 g/day through ten weeks; minimal real-world training/competition effect.
- **Bicarbonate repeated swims:** [Mero et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-10-52): 13 competitive male swimmers, 0.3 g/kg 60 min beforehand, two 100-m swims separated by 12 min; attenuation of second-swim slowing, rather than improved first swim.
- **Stacks are not additive:** [2024 endurance-trained trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38646853/): caffeine 3 mg/kg, bicarbonate 0.3 g/kg and creatine conditions. Caffeine improved 6-min TT mean power; bicarbonate/creatine did not confer automatic additive benefit.
- **New bicarbonate gel, experimental:** [Norte et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42788859/), DOI 10.1111/sms.70373, October issue; PubMed record retrieved 7 October 2026: 11 trained male cyclists, 3 h cycling with 80 g/h carbohydrate, with/without total 18 g sodium bicarbonate +2 g potassium bicarbonate. Subsequent 12-min TT improved by 12 W; 6-s sprint and 2-min TT did not significantly improve. New product-specific small trial, not evidence for homemade or routine during-event bicarbonate. Product supplied by manufacturer; expert-review only.
- **Sleep trade-off:** [Gardiner et al., online 2024 / issue 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsae230): 23 men, 100/400 mg caffeine at 12/8/4 h before bed; 400 mg disrupted aspects of sleep even 12 h earlier. The 100-mg group null finding is not proof of a universal safe bedtime cutoff, particularly outside this male sample.

## Popular interventions: what not to automate

- **Ketone esters/salts: C for routine performance.** Expensive, heterogeneous findings and possible GI effects; cannot replace carbohydrate. A 2026 human cycling trial found reduced exogenous carbohydrate oxidation/oxidation efficiency with ketone monoester despite 120 g/h carbohydrate. [Primary 2026 trial](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.01072.2025). Do not convert laboratory metabolic changes into a training plan.
- **BCAA/leucine alone, HMB, magnesium in replete athletes, phosphate: C** for routine sport-performance additions under AIS framework. Medical correction of deficiency is a separate indication. [AIS Group C](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_c).
- **Tart cherry, collagen/gelatin, omega-3, probiotics, curcumin: B/C depending on the specific endpoint.** Possible recovery, tissue or illness-management contexts are not demonstrated universal race-performance benefits. Strain/product/protocol specificity matters for probiotics. No routine performance dose without a defined indication and a dedicated evidence review. [AIS Group B](https://www.ausport.gov.au/ais/nutrition/supplements/group_b).
- **Vitamin D, calcium and other micronutrients: M/food-gap management**, not automatic megadosing. Blood tests or diet/clinical assessment as appropriate; clinical thresholds vary.
- **Glycerol:** AIS-supported specialized hyperhydration context, not ordinary hydration default. Exclude from automatic output because it needs its own protocol, GI/headache and fluid-balance review.
- **“Fat burners,” testosterone boosters, SARMs/prohormones, stimulant blends, unverified proprietary pre-workouts: X.** Banned/high-risk ingredients and unknown doses dominate any advertised benefit.
- **Hydrogels:** do not claim every hydrogel is superior to conventional carbohydrate at matched dose; carbohydrate amount, composition and individual tolerance matter.

## Sport application matrix (inference from the evidence above)

| Sport / session | Fueling logic | Supplement scope and uncertainty |
|---|---|---|
| Run: acceleration/max-velocity sprint | Food and glycogen availability around the full training day; no gel needed during a single short sprint. Repeated rounds/warm-up duration and short recovery change needs. | Creatine may support strength/repeated work; direct single race effect uncertain. Bicarbonate/beta-alanine should not be attached to every 60-/100-m event. |
| Run: 400 m / middle distance | Short event, substantial glycolytic contribution; pre-event tolerance, rounds and recovery are more relevant than hourly race feeding. | Selected buffering-aid rationale, but event/training-level-specific evidence required; mass and GI trade-offs matter. |
| Run: distance / long run | Duration/intensity/tolerance determine carb band; rehearse at running intensity, not only while cycling. | Caffeine is optional; >90 g/h is advanced and potentially GI-limited. Creatine benefit to supporting strength is not a faster-marathon claim. |
| Bike: endurance / road / time trial | More practical eating access; high workload/race duration can justify higher intake than an easy ride. | 120 g/h durability evidence relevant but no blanket threshold. Preserve fresh-versus-fatigued performance distinction. |
| Swim: pool sprint / endurance training | Fuel before, between sets/rounds and after; bottle/food on deck as allowed. Full session duration differs from race duration. | Swimming-specific null studies must remain visible. Extrapolate cautiously from cycle ergometry. |
| Open-water swim | Pre-event and planned permitted feeding opportunities; safe access/crew and environmental considerations. | Never invent feeds where course rules or safe access do not permit them. |
| Triathlon | Use total event duration and segment-specific plans. Usually more intake opportunity on bike, run often less tolerable; do not “catch up” all missed swim intake in a giant bolus. | Full-race efficacy is less certain than isolated cycling outcomes. Rehearse bike-to-run tolerance and cumulative caffeine. |
| HYROX | Fueling according to actual projected duration/intensity and safe access; pre-event food often more important than complicated race feeding. | Caffeine/creatine-strength/buffering evidence is extrapolated. A 2026 observational microbiome study of ten HYROX athletes cannot establish supplement or race-performance causality. [Study](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2026.1868776). |
| Supporting strength | Daily energy and protein plus carbohydrate for workload; after-session feeding based on rest of day and next session. | Creatine strongest routine optional ergogenic candidate; do not require powders if diet covers needs. |

## Sex, athlete level, low energy availability and anti-doping

**Sex and training level:** Many intervention studies are small and male-dominated; elite responses are not interchangeable with recreational responses. Female-inclusive swim studies help but do not solve gaps in long-event high-dose fueling, pregnancy, menopause, hormonal contraception or supplement interactions. Record population applicability. The [ISSN female-athlete statement 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2023.2204066) emphasizes energy adequacy and individual physiology; it is not authority to invent sex-only carbohydrate or sodium percentages. Menstrual symptoms, blood loss and health history can inform assessment when voluntarily provided. Do not diagnose hormonal state from calendar dates.

**Low energy availability:** Persistent under-fueling can compromise health and performance in athletes of any sex/body size. Recurrent bone stress injury, menstrual disruption, reduced libido, persistent fatigue/illness, restrictive eating or unexplained performance decline warrant qualified evaluation. These are nonspecific signals, not an app diagnosis. Do not apply a universal 30 or 45 kcal/kg fat-free-mass threshold diagnostically, infer energy availability from BMI, or recommend intentional carbohydrate restriction when warning signs exist. [IOC REDs 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994). Avoid “earn your food” logic and do not subtract exercise carbohydrates from recovery meals automatically.

**Anti-doping:** Check the list effective on the competition date and governing body. Ingredient permissibility does not verify product safety. Neither WADA nor USADA approves supplements. Third-party sport certification reduces, never eliminates, contamination risk. Verify exact product, flavor and batch/lot in the certifier database; save verification date and lot. [USADA Supplement Connect](https://www.usada.org/substances/supplement-connect/), [USADA batch-verification advisory](https://www.usada.org/athlete-advisory/verify-third-party-logos-supplements/), [WADA 2026 Prohibited List](https://www.wada-ama.org/sites/default/files/2025-09/2026list_en_final_clean_september_2025.pdf). A future list can be published before its effective date; do not confuse publication with applicability.

## Executable-rule specification (proposed behavior, not implemented code)

### Inputs and provenance

Required for any session-specific advice: sport; event/session type; planned duration; intensity category; objective; prior meal timing; next demanding session interval; known GI tolerance; food/fluid access. For mass-based numbers: measured/reported body mass with unit and date. For hydration arithmetic: observed pre/post mass, exercise duration, fluid intake, urine, conditions and measurement method. For supplement suggestions: age category, health/medication restrictions, use history, competition/anti-doping context and user preference; missing medical details should not be silently treated as “healthy.”

Every output record should include rule ID; rationale; human-readable range; input provenance; evidence IDs; population match; uncertainty; exclusions; reassessment trigger; units. Missing data remain null, with the smallest useful request for input. Do not generate a number by substituting a sample body mass, sex average, presumed sweat sodium or invented race time.

### Decision order

1. **NUT-00 safety gate:** known relevant disease/medication, pregnancy/lactation, minor athlete, persistent serious GI symptoms, suspected disordered eating/REDs → food-first general education and professional evaluation; no automatic performance-supplement dose. Emergency symptoms → emergency guidance rather than calculator output.
2. **NUT-01 baseline:** confirm adequate meals/energy/carbohydrate/protein before supplements. Daily carbohydrate ranges must be labeled workload planning; never infer body-composition targets.
3. **NUT-02 during fueling:** map duration+intensity to the matrix; show overlapping range and rationale. Prior fueling, full-session duration and recovery demand can modify relevance. Do not turn duration cutoffs into mandatory step changes.
4. **NUT-03 high-intake gate:** >60 g/h → mixed transportable carbohydrates should be considered; >90 g/h → explicit advanced status, established tolerance, competition rehearsal, qualified oversight and appropriate workload. >120 g/h → no automated recommendation. The maximum is a product safety/design boundary, not a claimed physiological ceiling.
5. **NUT-04 gut practice:** start at known tolerated strategy; test the planned source/quantity/fluid/timing in similar sport and conditions, log symptoms and completion. Increase only after tolerance; no universally validated “+10 g/h each week” algorithm. Symptoms require adjustment/review, not forced progression.
6. **NUT-05 recovery:** short turnaround → rapid glycogen-restoration option if mass known; otherwise explain without fabricated grams. Longer turnaround → ordinary sufficient meals. Count protein and exercise products in total intake.
7. **HYD-01 measured estimate:** validate units/time/intake/output; compute approximate sweat loss/rate, label limitations and conditions. Negative/implausible results → flag measurement issue or possible overdrinking, not clamp to a fake sweat rate.
8. **HYD-02 drinking plan:** never assign default fluid by sex or body mass. Without relevant measurements, offer thirst/access guidance. With measurements, a provisional experienced/practitioner plan must accommodate tolerable intake, environment and event access; it must not target weight gain or force 100% replacement.
9. **HYD-03 sodium:** loss calculation available only with credible concentration measurement; no calculated loss becomes a recommended replacement. Default result is “context-dependent; no universal target,” with an explanation. Include sodium in food, drink, salt capsules and bicarbonate when evaluating total exposure.
10. **SUP-01 optionality:** supplements default to off. Present conditional evidence, cost, downside and alternative. No “essential stack.”
11. **SUP-02 body-mass gate:** unknown mass blocks exact caffeine/bicarbonate/loading calculations, but does not block information about study ranges. Fixed-dose creatine research may be explained without inventing mass; still requires safety and preference gates.
12. **SUP-03 caffeine:** quantify all sources and timing, discuss sleep and prior adverse response; no automatic late-event top-ups or ≥6 mg/kg target. Unknown product caffeine content blocks exact cumulative-dose calculation.
13. **SUP-04 iron:** never recommend starting solely from demographic/fatigue data. Confirmed clinician-managed deficiency → describe/record the clinician plan, avoid changing dose. No auto-prescribing from an isolated ferritin number.
14. **SUP-05 traceability:** competition athlete → verified contemporary ingredient status and exact batch certification before calling a product lower-risk. If unavailable, say unverified; never “doping-safe.”
15. **POP-01 sex/level:** use measured workload, body mass where justified and individual tolerance; never multiply grams or supplement doses solely by sex. Clearly mark male-only/elite-only trial evidence. Optional health observations inform referrals, not unsupported physiology estimates.
16. **REV-01 reassess:** change in duration/intensity/sport, heat, GI response, medications, training phase or product formulation invalidates reuse of a prior strategy without review.

### Acceptance tests for a later implementation

- 30-minute easy run, fed adult, no mass: no mandatory gels, caffeine or sodium; no made-up mass.
- 65-minute hard effort: optional small carbohydrate/mouth-exposure strategy; not automatically 60–80 g/h.
- Three-hour bike with low feeding experience: conventional range plus practice; never jumps straight to 120 g/h from duration alone.
- Three-hour event with documented high-intake practice: advanced 90–120 g/h can be discussed with applicability and GI trade-offs, no promised race-time percentage.
- Same workload/tolerance, different sex labels: no arbitrary fueling/sodium change.
- 60-minute swim race but 3-hour meet/session: considers warm-up, rounds and recovery access without suggesting eating while actively swimming.
- Triathlon: segment sums and total exposure correct; no instantaneous catch-up feeding at transition.
- No sweat sodium measurement: sodium loss stays unknown. A measured loss of 900 mg/h does not become “take 900 mg/h.”
- Weight gain during prolonged exercise plus confusion/headache: urgent medical assessment, not more water or a salt correction calculator.
- Recovery deficit 1 kg: if rapid restoration is appropriate, quantity is based on deficit, never 1.25–1.5 L per kg whole-body mass.
- Missing mass plus caffeine request: research range and request for mass/health context; never assumes 70 kg.
- Creatine selected for single elite swim sprint: display uncertain/direct-null evidence even though supporting-strength evidence is strong.
- Beta-alanine selected one hour before first-ever race: no acute ergogenic claim.
- Iron requested because female/vegan/tired: assessment pathway, no automatic dose.
- Supplement label displays a testing logo without verified lot: no certification claim.
- HYROX selection: all extrapolated nutrition/supplement claims visibly labeled; no invented HYROX RCT.

### Additional clinical and protocol guardrails

- The health restrictions above are **review gates**, not claims that every listed condition is an absolute contraindication. In particular, prescribed bicarbonate can be used medically in kidney disease; that does not establish safety of a large performance load without the treating clinician.
- Nitrate protocols depend on oral nitrate-reducing bacteria. Antibacterial mouthwash can interfere; do not tell users to stop prescribed dental care. Refer to the treating professional if this conflicts with an attempted nitrate strategy. [Nitrate expert consensus](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01701-3).
- Sodium and salt units must be distinct: sodium chloride is approximately 39.3% sodium by mass; sodium bicarbonate approximately 27.4%. Never label grams of either compound as grams of sodium. These are chemical conversions, not intake targets.
- Avoid low-carbohydrate/fasted “train-low” blocks as a default performance enhancement. Adaptive signaling is not the same as improved competition performance; insufficient energy/carbohydrate and inability to complete the intended workload are meaningful downsides. [IOC carbohydrate review](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2011.585473), [IOC REDs 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994).

## Audit notes

- Sources were searched and read via publisher, PubMed, sports-governing/anti-doping and official professional pages. Exact source publication dates were preferred over inconsistent search-engine relative date labels.
- 2026 UCI race-nutrition review and 2026 primary carbohydrate/bicarbonate studies are newer than the frequently cited 2016/2018 general statements; use them to qualify, not silently replace, mature consensus.
- The October 2026 bicarbonate-gel paper is explicitly experimental. Its potassium/sodium bicarbonate mixture and ingestion schedule cannot be substituted by an ordinary bicarbonate kitchen recipe.
- This specification intentionally does not calculate a personal meal plan, prescribe supplements or change code.
